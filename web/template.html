Dark Vessel Finder

A dark-ship alert is a radar detection that no AIS report explains. Matched naively, two radar effects make honest ships look dark: moving ships are drawn hundreds of metres from where they really are, and big ships cast faint ghost copies kilometres away. This pipeline corrects for both and measures what that does to the alert list.

Why naive matching lies

Moving ships are drawn in the wrong place

A ship's motion towards or away from the radar adds Doppler, and focusing puts that Doppler at a different azimuth position:

Δy = −(R / V) · vg · sin θ

With R = __R_KM__ km, V = 7.6 km/s and θ = 30°, a 12-knot ship crossing the beam lands about __SHIFT12__ m along-track from its true position. A 24-knot patrol craft lands near __SHIFT24__ m. Using AIS speed and course, the pipeline predicts where the ship will appear, not only where it is.

Bright ships cast ghosts

The azimuth antenna pattern is sampled at the PRF, so energy folds back as a weaker copy at a fixed along-track offset:

Δyamb = λ · R · PRF / (2V) ≈ __AMB__ m

At an ambiguity-to-signal ratio of −18 dB a 250 m tanker's ghost still clears the CFAR threshold, and it has no AIS because nothing is there. The pipeline drops a detection when a detection at least 10 dB brighter sits one ambiguity offset away in azimuth.

Two more fixes help: report age sets the matching gate, so a 12-minute-old report from a fast ship gets a wider gate than a fresh report from an anchored one, and an optimal one-to-one assignment replaces nearest-neighbour, so two crossing ships cannot both claim the same report.

What each step buys

30 scenes per sea state, 45 ships each, 6 to 25% of ships dark (fishing and small craft most often), 3% of AIS reports spoofed by 2 to 5 km. Each step is added on top of the one before it.

Where it still fails

Small boats are not detected at all

Detector recall by ship length (moderate sea):

Boats under 20 m cover two to four pixels and sit near the clutter level. They are also the vessels most likely to fish illegally with AIS off. This is the same tiny-object problem I worked on at DRDO. There, replacing IoU with a Wasserstein-distance match score took tiny-object matching from 0.14 to 0.72, and super-resolution with tiled inference cut missed detections. A learned detector trained on AIS-matched detections as free labels is the next step.

Known limits of this study

  • Every number here comes from a simulator I wrote. The relative gains are the point, not the absolute rates.
  • Flat-earth geometry, one incidence angle per scene, no wakes, no along-track defocus, no land or coastline.
  • Ghost suppression can hide a real boat sitting exactly one ambiguity offset from a large ship. A careful adversary could use that.
  • Rough sea: the remaining false alerts are clutter spikes, which association cannot fix. That needs a better detector, not better matching.

Next steps on real data

  • Run on xView3-SAR (Sentinel-1 scenes with AIS-derived dark-vessel labels) to replace simulated numbers with measured ones.
  • Read R, V and incidence per pixel from the product annotation, so the Doppler correction varies across the swath.
  • Use the satellite's own AIS receiver timing. On Varuna, radar and AIS share a pass, which shrinks report age, and that is where most of the dead-reckoning error comes from.
  • Ask the analyst-facing question in plain language ("dark ships over 100 m inside the EEZ in the last pass") and answer it with fixed, testable query functions.

Questions I would like to ask the team

  • Does Varuna's compact reflectarray antenna push the ambiguity ratio above or below −18 dB, and does ghost handling happen on board or on the ground?
  • With 30-minute revisit, do you plan to link detections across passes, so that a ship that goes dark between passes is flagged on its track history rather than on one image?
